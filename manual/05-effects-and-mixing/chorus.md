# Chorus
> Two to four detuned voices, and a drift that stops them agreeing about the tuning.

The gap the first nine effects left: a flanger at low feedback is not a chorus, it is a flanger somebody turned down. A chorus is *voices* - several taps at different delays, each swept by its own phase of the LFO - so what comes back is a section rather than a doubling.

## The controls

- **rate** - how fast the voices sweep, as a note value, so it stays with the song.
- **depth** - how far each one moves. Small is a thickening; large is seasick.
- **voices** - two, three or four. More is not always better: two is a doubling, four is an ensemble and hides the part it is on.
- **spread** - how far apart the voices sit in the stereo field.
- **drift** *(extra)* - a slow random walk added to each voice's delay, which is what a bucket-brigade line does when its clock is not quite steady. At nothing it is a clean digital chorus; turned up, no two voices agree about the tuning for very long.
- **mix** - wet against dry.

## Using it well

**Voices, then depth.** If a chorus sounds artificial, the usual cause is too much depth on too few voices. Three voices at a small depth sounds like several players; one voice at a large depth sounds like a broken tape.

**Drift is what makes it analogue.** A digital chorus is perfectly periodic, and the ear hears the period. A little drift breaks that up and the effect stops announcing itself.

**It is wide by default**, so on something that has to stay centred - a lead, a bass - pull `spread` down and let the depth do the work.
